How to choose the right circular saw

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

For most DIYers and pros in 2026, a cordless circular saw with a brushless motor and a 6-½‑inch or 7-¼‑inch blade is the sweet spot. Focus on blade quality, bevel capacity, and how the saw feels in your hand rather than chasing peak power numbers.

What size blade should I choose?

The two most common blade diameters are 7‑¼ inches and 6‑½ inches. A 7-¼‑inch saw handles thicker stock, up to about 2-½ inches at 90 degrees, and is the standard for framing and demolition. The trade-off is weight and a slightly larger footprint, which can feel bulky for overhead or one-handed cuts. A 6-½‑inch saw is lighter, more compact, and easier to control for finish work, plywood, and smaller dimensional lumber. It cuts roughly 2 inches deep at 90 degrees, enough for most home projects. Many carpenters own both sizes, but if you only buy one, a 7-¼‑inch model is the more versatile choice for general remodeling.

Corded or cordless: which is right for you?

Corded saws deliver consistent, unlimited run time and slightly higher torque per dollar, ideal for production cutting, thick hardwood, or long workdays on a job site. Look for a model with at least a 15‑amp motor; these will slice through pressure‑treated lumber and LVL beams without bogging down. Cordless saws have improved dramatically. Brushless motors and high‑output batteries now give you nearly the same power as a corded saw for the first few cuts, and modern battery platforms (Makita LXT, DeWalt FlexVolt, Milwaukee M18 Fuel) run long enough for a full day of moderate use. The real advantage is mobility: no extension cord to trip over, easy roof work, and quick cuts in tight spaces. For most weekend warriors and even many pros, a cordless saw is now the primary tool.

What features make a circular saw easy to use?

The bevel adjustment matters more than you might think. Look for a saw with a clear, readable scale and a bevel stop at 45 degrees, ideally with positive stops at common angles (0°, 22.5°, 45°). A tool‑free bevel lever is standard on most mid‑range saws now, but some budget models still require a hex key, which slows you down. Good sightlines are critical. An open‑bottom design (often called a “see‑through” shoe) lets you watch the blade track your cut line. Electric brakes are a must for both safety and convenience, they stop the blade within seconds after you release the trigger. A dust‑port adapter or built‑in dust collection makes a huge difference for indoor or finished‑space work.

How do I choose a circular saw for specific projects?

For rough framing and deck building, prioritize depth of cut and durability. A 7-¼‑inch corded saw with a magnesium shoe (lightweight but tough) and a powerful motor is your best bet. A worm‑drive or rear‑handle saw can also be a plus here, as they offer more torque and a line‑of‑sight that many framers prefer. For finish carpentry and fine woodworking, a 6-½‑inch cordless saw with a fine‑tooth combi‑blade (or a dedicated plywood blade) will give you smooth, chip‑free cuts. Look for a model with a sturdy aluminum base that won’t flex, and a smooth depth‑adjustment knob. If you do a lot of ripping sheet goods, consider a saw with a dust‑port that can connect to a shop vac.

How much should I expect to spend?

Prices range widely. Budget‑friendly corded saws from established brands (Skil, Ryobi, Porter‑Cable) can be had for well under a hundred dollars, adequate for occasional use, but the build quality and bevel accuracy often reflect the price. Mid‑range cordless kits (tool plus battery and charger) typically run a few hundred dollars, and this is where you get the best value: brushless motors, electric brake, and decent ergonomics. Pro‑grade models from Makita, DeWalt, and Milwaukee push toward the higher end of the price spectrum, sometimes more for a bare tool. You’re paying for tighter tolerances, better dust management, longer battery life, and components that survive daily drops. For most users, a mid‑range cordless saw from a reputable brand is the smartest choice.

Frequently asked questions

Can a circular saw replace a miter saw?

No, they serve different purposes. A circular saw makes straight, freehand cuts; a miter saw excels at precise angled crosscuts and can be far more accurate and repeatable for trim work. For framing, a circular saw is essential, but a miter saw is a better choice for finish carpentry.

What’s the difference between a worm-drive and a sidewinder saw?

Worm-drive saws have the motor mounted in line with the blade, delivering more torque and a narrow profile that lets you see the cut line from above. They are heavier and slower to spin up. Sidewinder (hypoid) saws are lighter, faster, and cheaper, with the motor mounted on the side, better for general carpentry and overhead work.

Do I need a circular saw with a laser guide?

No. Laser guides can be helpful in dim light, but they are often imprecise after a drop and many users end up ignoring them. A well-designed saw with a clear notch or cut line mark on the shoe is more reliable. Save the money for a better blade or a quality square.

How deep can a circular saw cut?

At 90 degrees, a 7-¼‑inch blade cuts about 2-½ inches deep; a 6-½‑inch blade cuts about 2 inches. At 45 degrees, those depths drop by roughly ¼ to ½ inch. Always check the saw’s spec sheet, some shallow‑depth models exist for specialty work.

Is a higher amp rating always better in a corded saw?

Generally, yes, within reason. A 15‑amp corded saw will handle most materials confidently, while a 12‑amp saw will struggle on thick hardwood or dense pressure‑treated lumber. But don’t obsess over amps alone, a dull blade on a 15‑amp saw will still perform poorly.

What is the best circular saw brand?

There is no single “best” brand. Makita, DeWalt, Milwaukee, and Bosch all make excellent saws with loyal followings. The best brand for you depends on your existing battery platform, the type of work you do, and which saw feels balanced in your hand. Try holding a few at a store before you buy.

In shortIn the end, the best circular saw for you is the one that fits your hand, matches your battery system (if cordless), and comes with a sharp blade. Don’t get distracted by peak power claims or obscure features, invest in blade quality and a comfortable grip, and you’ll make better cuts for years.
